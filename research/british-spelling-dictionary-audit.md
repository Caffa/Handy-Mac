# US→British Spelling Dictionary Audit — Dated/Wrong Target Verdicts

Date: 2026-11-14. Companion to the reflection→reflexion removal (already decided).

## Numbered candidates

### 1. inflexion (vs inflection) — REMOVE
- Fowler's Dictionary of Modern English Usage, 4th ed: "current usage prefers -ction (e.g. connection, deflection, genuflection, inflection)".
- Routledge Handbook of the English Writing System: -xion forms "appea[r] old-fashioned or even archaic to British readers".
- Corpus data (Sapling): UK prefers inflection 69–31; Collins marks inflexion as "in BRIT, also use" variant; Cambridge lists inflection as headword with "(UK also inflexion)".
- Same logic applies to connexion (Times dropped it in the 1970s).
- Sources: https://english.stackexchange.com/questions/342402/difference-between-inflection-and-inflexion ; https://sapling.ai/usage/inflection-vs-inflexion ; https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/inflection

### 2. adaptor (vs adapter) — KEEP
- Collins lists adaptor as the British headword ("adaptor in British English or adapter"). Oxford Learner's: adapter "(also adaptor)".
- Guardian style guide distinguishes: "adapter someone who adapts; adaptor plug".
- Usage data: adapter leads even in UK, but adaptor remains fully standard, current, and preferred for electrical plugs. Not dated.
- Sources: https://www.collinsdictionary.com/dictionary/english/adaptor ; https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/adapter ; https://writingexplained.org/adapter-vs-adaptor-difference (Guardian cite)

### 3. kilogramme / kilogrammes (vs kilogram) — REMOVE
- Wikipedia: "In the United Kingdom both spellings are used, with 'kilogram' having become by far the more common."
- Cambridge/Collins treat kilogramme as a secondary variant; ELU commenters: "I haven't seen the 'gramme' spelling for years"; British scientific journals always used gram/kilogram.
- Converting kilogram→kilogramme produces dated French-style spelling; SI/symbol kg context makes it worse.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilogram ; https://english.stackexchange.com/questions/377274/kilograms-or-kilogrammes-british-english ; https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/kilogram

### 4. tzar (vs tsar) — REMOVE
- Tsar is the standard British and international form: OALD headword tsar "(also tzar, czar)"; Collins: "tsar in British English or czar… Also (less commonly): tzar"; Cambridge: "mainly UK (also tzar)".
- Wiktionary/wikidiff: "Tsar is the usual spelling today, about 20 times as common as tzar"; tzar tagged "Rare".
- The dictionary converts tsar→tzar, i.e. toward the rare variant — backwards. Remove.
- Sources: https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/tsar ; https://www.wikidiff.com/tzar/tsar ; https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/tsar

### 5. syphon (vs siphon) — KEEP
- Cambridge: siphon "(also syphon)". Longman: syphon = "a British spelling of siphon".
- Simon Horobin (Oxford, "Spelling Trouble" blog): the etymologically wrong syphon "is now widely accepted and frequently features as a variant spelling in dictionaries".
- Sapling country data: UK prefers syphon 61–39 (the only country that does); siphon still dominant globally and in formal/technical writing.
- Verdict: living, common British variant — not dated. KEEP.
- Sources: https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/siphon ; https://www.ldoceonline.com/dictionary/syphon ; http://spellingtrouble.blogspot.com/2014/06/siphon-or-syphon.html ; https://sapling.ai/usage/siphon-vs-syphon

### 6. philtre (vs philter) — KEEP (note: word itself is literary)
- Philtre is the standard British spelling of the word: Collins "philtre in British English, or US philter"; OALD "philtre (US English philter)"; MW "chiefly British philtre".
- The word itself is literary/rare (love potion — OALD tags it "(literary)"), but the dictionary only converts spelling, not register; if the source says "philter", "philtre" is the correct BrE rendering.
- Sources: https://www.collinsdictionary.com/dictionary/english/philtre ; https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/philtre ; https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/philter

### 7. whiskey→whisky — REMOVE
- The split is origin-based, not US-vs-UK: Scotch/Japanese/Canadian = whisky (legally protected: Scotch Whisky Regulations 2009 uses only "whisky"); Irish and most American = whiskey even in British publications.
- "Irish whiskey" is spelled with the e in UK media too; blanket conversion corrupts it. "Scottish whiskey" is simply incorrect.
- EU Regulation 2019/787 names the category "whisky or whiskey"; Irish technical file permits both but "whiskey" is customary.
- Sources: https://www.wsetglobal.com/knowledge-centre/blog/2024/whisky-or-whiskey-is-there-a-difference-and-does-it-matter ; https://www.casktrade.com/buying-selling/whisky-investment-guides/whisky-or-whiskey-the-real-difference ; https://www.bbcgoodfood.com/howto/guide/whisky-or-whiskey-whats-difference
- (Guardian style guide keeps the same distinction under its W entries.)

### 8. foetus/foetal (vs fetus/fetal) — KEEP for general text; medical contexts differ
- Guardian style guide (current online edition): "foetus not fetus" (and "foetid not fetid"). https://www.theguardian.com/guardian-style-guide-f
- Oxford (lexicography): fetus is now standard in technical/medical use "throughout the English-speaking world, but foetus is still found in British English outside technical contexts". https://separatedbyacommonlanguage.blogspot.com/2015/05/foetus-and-foetal-and-bit-on.html
- Medical bodies have flipped: NICE style guide — "Do use Fetus / Do not use Foetus" (https://www.nice.org.uk/corporate/ecd1/chapter/spelling-and-choosing-the-right-word); BMJ and The Lancet use fetus/fetal; NHS general pages are mixed.
- Corpus trend: UK now prefers fetus 75–25, fetal 84–16 (Sapling).
- Verdict: for a general US→UK transcription dictionary, KEEP foetus/foetal (Guardian-endorsed general BrE). Caveat: any medical/technical text should stay fetus/fetal — consider a context note.

### 9. spelt (vs spelled) — spelled is fully standard BrE; forcing spelt is style-only → REMOVE the spelled→spelt pair
- Cambridge: "spelled or UK also spelt" — both forms listed for UK. Scribbr: "In UK English, both 'spelled' and 'spelt' are commonly used." BrE ngram: spelled overtook spelt around 1980.
- Moreover the -ed form is standard even in BrE for the "signified" sense ("spelled trouble/disaster/ruin"), so blanket conversion can produce non-idiomatic output.
- Sources: https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/spell (as cited in https://english.stackexchange.com/questions/5712/spelt-vs-spelled) ; https://www.scribbr.co.uk/uk-vs-us/spelled-or-spelt ; https://writingexplained.org/spelt-or-spelled-difference

### 10. Sanity-KEEP list — all confirmed fine modern British
sceptic, maths, pyjamas, aeroplane, moustache, doughnut, omelette, titbit, pernickety, cognisance, furore, skilful, wilful, behove, judgement, liquorice, carburettor, calliper, paediatric, palaeontology, haemoglobin, manoeuvre, plough, mould, sulphur, aluminium.
- Spot-checked against Collins/Cambridge/Oxford: titbit (Collins: "titbit in British English, US tidbit"), pernickety (Collins: British, "in AM use persnickety"), sulphur (Oxford Learner's: "sulphur still remains the usual spelling in British, Irish, South African and Indian English" — note IUPAC/RSC use sulfur in scientific nomenclature), judgement (standard general BrE; UK legal writing prefers judgment).
- None dated. KEEP all.
- Sources: https://www.collinsdictionary.com/dictionary/english/titbit ; https://www.collinsdictionary.com/dictionary/english/pernickety ; https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/sulphur

## Tail-section scan — no other genuine problems
All remaining pairs map US form → current standard BrE. Checked: accoutre/accoutrement, anaemia, archaeology, artefact, axe, baulk, behove, calibre, chilli, cognisance, cosy, crayfish, distil, enamour, endeavour, furore, glamour, goitre, kidnapper/kidnapping, liquorice, maths, omelette, pedlar, pernickety, speciality, succour, titbit, woollen, worshipper, instilment, enrolment, instalment, plough, snowplough, reconnoitre, saltpetre — all standard current BrE (Collins/Cambridge/Oxford). Minor notes:
- instilment: valid BrE but rare word itself (fine to keep; nothing better).
- speciality: standard BrE, but note UK healthcare writing (NHS) often uses "specialty" — do not convert in medical transcripts if avoidable; harmless in general text.
- kidnaper/kidnaping: correct US archaic sources; kidnapping/kidnapper are universal.

## Final verdict summary
REMOVE:
- inflexion (→ leave inflection)
- kilogramme / kilogrammes (→ leave kilogram)
- tsar→tzar (→ leave tsar)
- whiskey→whisky (origin-dependent; never convert Irish/American whiskey)
- spelled→spelt (spelled is fully standard BrE; style-only, wrong in 'signify' sense)

FLAG (uncertain / conditional):
- foetus/foetal — KEEP for general BrE (Guardian), but medical style (NICE/BMJ/Lancet, and most UK corpus usage) now mandates fetus/fetal; add a medical-context exception or note.
- syphon — KEEP (still common BrE variant, UK-only preference) but note siphon is the international/technical standard if that matters for the use case.
- philtre — KEEP spelling conversion; word itself is literary/rare.
- sulphur — KEEP for general BrE; scientific nomenclature (IUPAC/RSC) uses sulfur.
- judgement — KEEP; UK legal writing uses judgment (no action needed for general text).
- speciality — KEEP; NHS/medical UK usage often "specialty" (context caveat only).
